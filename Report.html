Key Findings

  • Males recorded higher hospitalised injury counts than females across car drivers, motorcyclists, and pedal cyclists throughout most years between 2011 and 2021.
  • Car driver hospitalisations remained relatively stable over time, with male injury counts consistently exceeding female counts.
  • Motorcyclists experienced some of the largest gender differences in hospitalisation counts among the selected road user groups.
  • Pedal cyclist hospitalisations were generally lower than car driver hospitalisations but still showed a clear difference between males and females.
  • The visualisations demonstrate how injury trends vary by road user type, gender, and year for Australians aged 40–64.
  • Car drivers recorded the highest number of hospitalised injuries among the three selected road user groups, making them the largest contributor to injury hospitalisations within the chosen age range.
  • Male car driver hospitalisations showed a generally consistent trend throughout the study period, suggesting that injury rates for this group remained relatively stable over time.
  • Female hospitalisation counts remained lower than male counts across all selected road user categories, indicating a persistent gender difference in injury-related hospital admissions.
  • The yearly data reveals that hospitalisation numbers fluctuated rather than continuously increasing or decreasing, highlighting the influence of changing road conditions, travel behaviour, and external factors over time.
  • Comparing multiple road user groups on the same website allows users to identify which forms of transport are associated with higher injury counts and observe how these patterns differ between males and females.

Data Notes

Victoria

From calendar year 2012, Victoria's data is not directly comparable with previous years due to a break in the series. Victoria changed case inclusion criteria to exclude cases cared for solely in Emergency Departments from 1 July 2012. National Injury Surveillance Unit (NISU - Flinders University) estimates this decreased admitted case counts in Australia by -5.6 per cent in 2012-13 compared to 2011-12. The estimated decrease in 2012 was approximately -2.8 per cent, with the reduction likely to differ by road user group.

NSW

A similar change in NSW occurred from June 2017. The emergency department admission policy was changed for New South Wales hospitals in 2017. Episodes of care delivered entirely within a designated emergency department or urgent care centre are no longer categorised as an admission. This narrowing of the inclusion has the effect of reducing the number of admissions recorded in the 2017 year and going forward. The impact of the change was felt disproportionately among hospitalisations for injury and poisoning. The change in NSW’s emergency department admission policy may have had different effects on case numbers within different external cause categories.
